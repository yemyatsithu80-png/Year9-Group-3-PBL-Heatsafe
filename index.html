Introduction

Welcome to our website, where you can learn about heat waves and how they affect our daily lives. A heat wave is a period of unusually hot weather that lasts for many days, often bringing temperatures much higher than normal. As the climate warms, heat waves are becoming more frequent, longer, and more intense in many parts of the world. Here, you can learn what causes heat waves, what dangers they bring, and how you can stay safe and protect your family, pets, and community during extreme heat.

Our Objective

Our aim is to raise awareness about the growing danger of heat waves and to help people understand how to stay safe during extreme heat. We want to encourage individuals, families, and communities to prepare in advance, recognize the warning signs of heat-related illness, and take simple but effective steps to protect themselves. By sharing clear information, we hope to reduce heat-related health problems and save lives. Everyone deserves to stay cool and safe, even when the temperature rises.

How hot is too hot?

Air temperature alone can mislead you. The heat index adds humidity to show how hot it feels in the shade. The US National Weather Service sorts it into four danger levels.

  1. Caution

    About 27 & 32C

    Tiredness is possible after long exposure or hard activity.

  2. Extreme caution

    About 33 & 39C

    Heat cramps and heat exhaustion are possible.

  3. Danger

    About 40 & 51C

    Heat exhaustion is likely and heat stroke is possible. Limit time outdoors.

  4. Extreme danger

    About 52C and above

    Heat stroke is highly likely. Stay somewhere cool.

Direct sunshine can make it feel several degrees hotter. Source: US National Weather Service heat index.

Heat stroke is an emergency

If someone is confused, has slurred speech, has hot dry skin, or faints, call your local emergency number straight away. Move them somewhere cool and cool their skin with water or wet cloths. Do not give drinks to someone who is confused or unconscious.

Why Heat Waves Matter

Health Risk

Extreme heat can cause dehydration, heat exhaustion, and deadly heat stroke. Children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with health conditions are most at risk.

Rising Temperatures

Climate change is making heat waves hotter, longer, and more frequent. Cities are hit hardest because concrete and buildings trap heat, even at night.

Environmental Damage

Heat waves dry out soil, reduce water supplies, cause wildfires, and harm animals, plants, and entire ecosystems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is a heat wave?

A heat wave is a period of abnormally hot weather that lasts for several days or longer, often with temperatures much higher than the usual average for that area and season.

Who is most at risk during a heat wave?

Elderly people, infants and young children, pregnant women, outdoor workers, athletes, and people with chronic illnesses (such as heart or lung conditions) are at the highest risk.

What is the difference between heat exhaustion and heat stroke?

Heat exhaustion causes heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, and nausea, but the person is still alert. Move them to a cool place, loosen their clothing, give small sips of water, and cool their skin with wet cloths. If they do not feel better within about an hour, get medical help. Heat stroke is a medical emergency — the body temperature rises above 40°C, the skin may be hot and dry, and the person may become confused or unconscious. Call your local emergency number immediately, and cool them down with water or wet cloths while you wait for help.

How much water should I drink during a heat wave?

Drink water regularly throughout the day — even if you don't feel thirsty — and drink more if you are sweating heavily. Pale yellow urine is a good sign; dark yellow urine means you need more fluids. Avoid alcohol and sugary drinks. If a doctor has limited how much fluid you can drink (for example because of kidney or heart disease), ask them how much is safe for you.

What should I do if my home has no air conditioning?

Close curtains during the day, open windows at night, use fans, take cool showers or apply damp cloths to your skin, wear light clothing, and visit a public cooling place (library, mall, community hall) for a few hours if you can. Fans alone are not enough when indoor temperatures are very high (above about 35°C).

What are the early warning signs of heat illness?

Heavy sweating, muscle cramps, headache, dizziness, nausea, and unusual tiredness. If you notice these, stop what you are doing, move somewhere cool, and drink water.

What are the hottest hours of the day?

Usually late morning to late afternoon, roughly 11 AM to 4 PM. Try to do outdoor tasks early in the morning or in the evening.
